Last week I stopped feeding the starter every twelve hours and started feeding it by ratio instead. Here is what happened, with the dead one left in the photos.

The rule I had been following was the one everyone gives: feed twice a day, equal weights of starter, flour and water. It worked in June. It stopped working in August, and I could not see why until I wrote down the kitchen temperature next to each feed. June was nineteen degrees. August was twenty-six. Same ratio, same clock, a starter that was peaking and collapsing hours before I got to it.

So I changed one thing. I kept the clock and changed the ratio: one part starter to five parts each of flour and water, by weight, instead of one to one to one. At twenty-two degrees that peaks in about eight hours in my kitchen. At twenty-six it is nearer six. The clock is a proxy for the ratio and the temperature. Use the real things.

Day four I came home to a grey layer of liquid on top. This is hooch. It is not a warning light; it is a fuel gauge, and it is reading empty. I stirred it back in, fed at one to five to five, and it peaked on schedule the next morning. The loaf was a little more sour. I will leave the dead one in the photos, but this was not it.

An aside on the fridge, because three people asked. Cold is a brake, not a pause button. Modernist Bread is good on why: fermentation slows with temperature rather than stopping. My own numbers, for what one kitchen is worth: five days between feeds in the fridge and it came back on the first feed; ten days and it needed two; twenty-four days and it needed four. I will find the page.

So: stop asking how long. Ask whether it has peaked. Put a rubber band round the jar at the level you fed it, and look at the dome. When the dome starts to flatten, that is the answer, whatever the clock says.

Next time, someone is going to ask me whether any of this makes better bread than a packet of yeast, and I owe them a real answer.
