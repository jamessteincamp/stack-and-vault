**Citation.** <Author(s), year, title, journal or publisher, volume/pages, DOI or URL.
A bare journal name and year is not a citation. If the claim names only "a study in
X", finding the real paper is the work.>

**What it says, in its own words.** <The sentence or two, or the figures, that the
zettels rest on — verbatim, in quotation marks, with units, the population measured
and the year. Short: a line or two, never a passage. This is the only place the
source's own wording survives, and it is what a zettel gets checked against later.>

<!-- The two fields above are the whole requirement. A source used honestly for its own
     population needs nothing more, and the fields below can say "none". They exist
     because the common failure mode is the overreach: the number is real and the
     population it describes is not the one being talked about. -->

**Claims it supports.** <One line per claim, quoted, with the work it appears in. A
list, because one source can carry several claims, and each may fit it differently.>

**Sample and scope.** <Who or what was measured, where, when, how many. This is where
most claims break: the finding is real and the population is not the one on the page.>

**Gap between the claims and the source.** <State it plainly, or write "none".
An honest gap is worth more than a confident overreach, and it is the thing a
critic will find first.>

**Strength.** <Systematic review / peer-reviewed study / official statistic /
statute / industry report / journalism / one person's count. Say which.
Where figures conflict, use the more conservative number, and state personal
observation as personal.>

**Contested by.** <Who disputed it and on what grounds, if anyone.>

**Zettels.** <The notes that rest on this.>
